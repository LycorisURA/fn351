Coupon 01 · Introduction

What a bond actually is

A bond is an IOU with a schedule attached. Everything else in this course is a way of arguing about what that schedule is worth today.

The definition moved

"Fixed income" used to mean the cash flows are fixed. It no longer does. Floating-rate notes, interest rate futures, caps and floors all have cash flows that move — yet they sit squarely in the asset class. The working definition today:

A fixed income security is one whose price is driven mainly by interest rates. So: a callable corporate bond is in. An FX spot position is not — its price is driven by the exchange rate, not by the discount curve.

Why bonds are the tractable half of finance

Compare the two valuation problems honestly:

Why this matters Because the cash flows are known, precision in the discount rate is the whole game. That is why an entire lecture goes to extracting the term structure, and why arbitrage arguments bite so hard here and not in equities.

Order the asset class by how certain its cash flows are, and you get the course syllabus in one line: Treasury bonds → Treasury derivatives → corporate bonds → convertibles → CMOs → equity.

The contract: what must be in it

  • Maturity — when par comes back and the contract ends.
  • Coupon rate — the periodic payment, quoted as a percentage of par.
  • Frequency — semiannual for US and Thai Treasuries and corporates.
  • Issuer — which determines credit, and therefore the spread.
  • Covenants and options — senior/junior, callable/putable, sinking funds.

Negative covenants prohibit (no asset sales, no additional borrowing); affirmative covenants promise (maintain ratios, pay on time). Redemption is either bullet — coupons then principal at maturity — or amortizing, principal returned throughout, which is why stated maturity is a poor risk measure for an amortizing security.

FabozziCh. 1

Credit risk is really three risks

Default is only the headline. A bond can lose value without anyone missing a payment:

  • Default risk — the issuer fails to pay on time.
  • Credit spread risk — the market demands a wider spread over Treasuries for that issuer or sector, so the price falls.
  • Downgrade risk — a rating agency cuts the rating, which widens the spread.

A guarantee from a private insurer does not remove any of this — the guarantor can be downgraded too, which is exactly what happened to bond insurers at the end of 2007. Add counterparty risk whenever the trade involves another party's promise: a leveraged position's lender, or an OTC derivative with no exchange standing in the middle.

FabozziCh. 1

Rate risk and reinvestment risk pull in opposite directions

Rising rates hurt the price of a bond you hold. Falling rates hurt the rate at which you can reinvest its coupons. Because the two risks offset, a portfolio can be built so they cancel over a chosen horizon — the idea behind immunization.

Reinvestment risk grows with the size and earliness of cash flows: high coupons, long horizons, and above all amortizing securities that return principal monthly. A call provision stacks three costs on the holder: cash flows become uncertain, the bond is taken away precisely when reinvestment rates are low, and the price is capped near the call price so the upside from falling rates is lost.

Maturity buckets in market usage: 1–5 years short, 5–12 intermediate, >12 long.

FabozziCh. 5

Why two bonds of the same maturity yield different amounts

The yield on any non-Treasury bond is the Treasury (base) rate plus a benchmark spread that pays for everything the Treasury does not carry: issuer type, credit, embedded options, taxability, liquidity, and even financeability — issues that are "special" in the repo market can be borrowed against cheaply, so their yields are bid down.

absolute spread = yA − yB    relative spread = yA − yByB    yield ratio = yAyB A 10-year AA corporate at 3.22% against a 2.53% Treasury: 69 bp absolute, 27% relative, ratio 1.27.
equivalent taxable yield = tax-exempt yield1 − marginal tax rate A 3.40% municipal is worth 3.40/(1 − 0.35) = 5.23% taxable to a 35% taxpayer, and 6.18% to a 45% taxpayer. The same bond is worth more to whoever is taxed more.

Options move spreads in both directions. A call favours the issuer, so investors demand a wider spread; a put or conversion right favours the investor, so the spread narrows — sometimes enough that a corporate bond yields less than the Treasury. That is why spreads for bonds with options are quoted option-adjusted.

TuckmanPart One

Why markets increasingly price off swaps, not government bonds

A benchmark needs to be liquid and to carry the rate information common to every fixed income price. Government bonds are liquid, but each issue is in fixed supply and has quirks — the on-the-run 10-year trades rich because it is the easiest to trade and to finance. A curve built from ordinary bonds therefore misprices the on-the-runs.

A swap has no such problem: a 10-year 4% swap can never be in short supply, because any two willing counterparties can write another one. Swaps are a fungible commodity; bonds are not. So swap traders strike one curve and price their whole book off it, and even in the US Treasury market strategists judge relative value by each issue's spread to the swap curve.

One detail that trips people up: coupon frequency is not universal. France and Germany pay government coupons annually; the US, Italy, Japan and the UK pay semiannually.

STRIPS: where the discount factors come from

In the early 1980s dealers noticed unmet demand for zeros longer than a year. They bought coupon bonds, put them in trust, and sold each cash flow separately. The Treasury took the idea in-house in 1985: Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal of Securities.

A 2% November 2029 note becomes ten small coupon claims plus one principal claim, each an independent zero-coupon bond, each clearing through the Fed's book-entry system. Standardised and liquid — which is exactly what makes them the market's source of observable discount factors.

Tax trap A STRIP pays nothing until maturity but is taxed annually on imputed interest (IRC §1286). The holder owes tax on income they have not received — which is why STRIPS live disproportionately in tax-deferred accounts.

TIPS: indexing the principal, not the coupon

The coupon rate is fixed. The principal is restated by CPI each period, and the fixed rate is applied to the restated principal — so the payment grows with inflation and so does the final redemption.

Repo: borrowing against collateral

A dealer sells a government security today and agrees to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. That price difference is the interest. Same trade, two names: it is a repo to the borrower who starts with the security, a reverse repo to the lender who starts with cash.

The lender's risk is that the collateral falls in value, so they lend less than the collateral is worth — the shortfall is the margin or haircut. Credit risk of the counterparty matters much less than it would on an unsecured loan, which is why the repo market clears in trillions overnight.

Short selling, and how repo makes it possible

Selling what you do not own: borrow the security through a dealer from another account, sell it, and post the proceeds plus margin. While short you owe the lending account every cash flow the security pays — coupons included. Close out by buying it back and returning it.

If the original owner sells, the broker must find the security in another account; if it cannot be found, the short is bought in — forced to close immediately at whatever the market asks. In the bond market this is usually done through repo: take the security in on a reverse repo, sell it into the market, and buy it back to return at the repurchase date.

Orders behave as they do in equities. A market order takes the inside quote; a limit order buys at or below, or sells at or above, its limit; a stop-loss sells once the price falls to the trigger; a stop-buy buys once it rises to it. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, and the spread is the dealer's compensation — a round trip costs the spread plus commission.

FabozziCh. 6

TIPS, in full

  • The coupon rate is the real rate, set at auction. The index is non-seasonally-adjusted CPI-U, applied with a three-month lag through a daily index ratio (reference CPI at settlement ÷ reference CPI at issue).
  • Principal is restated every six months; the coupon is half the real rate times the restated principal.
  • Deflation floor: at maturity you receive the greater of the inflation-adjusted principal and original par.
  • Tax drag: the principal adjustment is taxed each year although it is not paid until maturity — which makes TIPS more attractive in tax-deferred accounts.
$100,000 of a 3.5% TIPS, 1.5% half-year inflation: principal → $101,500; coupon = 1.75% × 101,500 = $1,776.25
next half-year at 1%: principal → $102,515; coupon = 1.75% × 102,515 = $1,794.01
FabozziCh. 6

How the Treasury market actually trades

  • On-the-run is the most recently auctioned issue at each maturity; everything it replaced is off-the-run. On-the-runs are the most liquid and trade at slightly lower yields.
  • New issues trade before they exist in the when-issued (wi) market, from announcement to issue date — quoted in yield, since the coupon is not set until the auction.
  • Dealers trade with each other anonymously through interdealer brokers, whose screens show the inside market.
  • Settlement is T+1. Treasuries are noncallable, held in book-entry form at the Fed, and their interest is exempt from state and local tax — part of why they yield less than taxable corporates.
  • The coupon on a new note is set in eighths of a percent, at the highest rate that keeps the price at or just below par at the auction's high yield.
FabozziCh. 6

Three yields for one bill

discount yield yd = F − PF × 360t     CD-equivalent = 360 · yd360 − t · yd A 100-day bill, $100,000 face, priced at $99,100: discount yield 3.24%, CD-equivalent 3.27%. The discount basis understates return twice over — it divides by face rather than the price paid, and it uses a 360-day year.

A puzzle worth being able to explain: dealers quote bills with the bid yield above the ask yield (say 5.91% bid, 5.89% ask). Nothing is wrong. Yield moves opposite to price, so the higher bid yield is the lower bid price.

Very short bills have occasionally been offered above face value. That is a negative yield — investors paying for the safety and liquidity of parking cash in Treasuries.

TuckmanCh. 1

Not all STRIPS are equal

Coupon STRIPS maturing on the same date are fungible: any of them can go toward rebuilding any bond's coupon. Principal STRIPS are not — only the P-STRIP stripped from a particular bond can reconstitute that bond's principal. So P-STRIPS inherit their parent bond's richness or cheapness.

In Tuckman's May 2010 data, the P-STRIP from the on-the-run 10-year traded about 2.16 points above the C-STRIP maturing the same day. Pricing long Treasuries with C-STRIPS alone missed that bond by 2.08; using C-STRIPS for coupons and each bond's own P-STRIP for principal cut every error in the sample to roughly a tenth of a point or less.

Fabozzi adds the tax angle: some foreign investors can treat a principal strip's accretion as a capital gain, so they prefer principal strips — another reason the two trade differently.

Three ways to build the replicating portfolio

Method 1

N zeros

One zero per cash flow date, face value set equal to that cash flow. Always works when the strips exist. The most mechanical, and the one that defines fair value.
Method 2

One coupon bond + one zero

Scale a same-maturity coupon bond until its coupons match, then plug the principal gap with a zero. To match 6% from 10%: hold 0.6 face of the 10% (coupons 30) and 400 of the 2-yr zero. Price: 0.6 × 1057.97 + 0.4 × 873.36 = 984.13.
Method 3

N coupon bonds

No zeros needed. Match the last cash flow first with the longest bond, then work backwards — each step has one unknown. Equivalently, invert the cash-flow matrix: F = CF⁻¹ · target.
Fn·Mn = Cn  →  Fn
Fn−1·Mn−1 + Fn·cn = Cn−1  →  Fn−1   … and so on back to F1 Back-substitution. Earlier bonds only ever add cash flow to dates already solved, so each equation has exactly one new unknown — which is why the matrix is triangular and always invertible when each date has a bond maturing on it.
Negative weights are normal Solving method 3 for the 6% bond gives F = (−0.0178, −0.0178, −0.0183, +0.9810). Three short positions and one long. A replicating portfolio is not required to be long everything — it is required to match cash flows.

The law of one price, and what to do when it breaks

Two portfolios with identical cash flows and identical risks must have the same price. "Risks" is doing real work in that sentence: credit, liquidity, counterparty. A Treasury strip and a corporate zero with the same cash flows are not the same security.

When A is cheaper than B: buy A, short B. Pocket the difference today; the future cash flows cancel exactly, so there is no liability left to worry about. If the market corrects before maturity you simply unwind — sell A, close the short in B, and the future is flat either way.

Arbitrage with a bid–ask spread

You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so the test becomes two one-sided comparisons, and you check both:

  • ask(A) < bid(B) → buy A, sell B.
  • ask(B) < bid(A) → buy B, sell A.

These can never both be true at once — that would require an ask below a bid. Finding an arbitrage "both ways" means you have made an arithmetic error.

Dominant securities

A second kind of arbitrage does not need identical cash flows — only a security that is superior in every respect yet cheaper. A putable bond dominates the otherwise-identical straight bond: the holder has a right they may never use but can never be hurt by.

price(putable) = price(straight) + price(the put)  ≥  price(straight) So if ask(putable) < bid(straight), buy the putable and short the straight. The lecture's numbers: putable ask 101:9 = 101.28, straight bid 102:0 = 102.00. Buy the better bond for less.

When the coupons differ you build the comparison first. To pit a 6-year 8% putable against a 6-year 10% straight and a 6-year zero: 0.8 of the 10% gives coupons of 4, and 0.2 of the zero fills the principal — a synthetic 8% straight bond whose bid is 0.8(102) + 0.2(71) = 95.80, against a putable ask of 95:02 = 95.06. Buy the putable, sell the synthetic.

You are never trapped Three endings, all safe. Market corrects → unwind, cash flow ≥ 0. Rates stay low, the put is never worth exercising → hold both to maturity, cash flows cancel. Rates rise → put the bond back at the exercise price and use the proceeds to buy in the short. The dominant security's optionality is what guarantees the third branch.

Before you call it free money

  • Liquidity and depth — the quote may be for 1mm, not 100mm.
  • Shorting may be impossible or expensive; a special repo rate can exceed the edge.
  • Financing — can you borrow, and at what rate?
  • Transaction costs beyond the spread: clearing, custody, capital charges.

TuckmanCh. 1 App.

Discounting is arbitrage pricing

Two procedures always give the same number: (1) extract discount factors from a set of bonds and discount the new bond's cash flows; (2) build the new bond's replicating portfolio from that same set and add up its cost. The proof is two lines of matrix algebra — the transpose of one expression is the other — so discounting is shorthand for the arbitrage argument, not an assumption layered on top.

C · F = ctarget  →  F = C−1 · ctarget     price = F′ · P = d′ · ctarget The one condition: the bonds must span the payment dates — at least one bond paying on every date. T bonds all maturing on the last date work; T bonds all maturing one period earlier do not, because nothing pays on the final date.
FabozziCh. 5–6

Stripping and reconstitution keep the spot curve honest

Price a 10% 10-year Treasury off the theoretical spot curve and it is worth about 115.42. Price it off its own 7.8% yield to maturity and you get 115.08. If the market used the single yield, a dealer would buy the bond, strip it into zeros, sell them, and pocket roughly 34 cents per 100. Buying pressure lifts the bond until the gap closes.

The trade runs the other way too. If a coupon bond is above the value of its zeros, buy the zeros and deliver them to the Treasury to rebuild the bond — reconstitution. Stripping and reconstituting between them pin coupon bonds to spot rates. It is why "each cash flow at its own rate" is how prices actually behave, not merely a textbook preference.

Coupon 04 · Term structure

One curve, three ways of saying it

Discount factors, spot rates and forward rates carry identical information. Knowing which form answers which question is most of the skill.

First, make rates comparable

10% compounded annually and 10% compounded daily are different prices. $1,000 grows to $1,100 under the first and 1000(1 + 0.1/365)365 = $1,105.20 under the second — the annual bank must offer 10.52% to compete.

(1 + rannual)T = (1 + rdaily365)365T = (1 + rsemi2)2T Convention: quote the period rate multiplied by the number of periods. The semiannual rate — the bond-equivalent yield — is the half-year rate × 2, and it is the default everywhere in this course. More frequent compounding always gives a lower quoted rate for the same money.

Spot rates

yt = 2 × [ (1d(t))1/(2t) − 1 ]   =   2 × [ (FBt)1/(2t) − 1 ] The spot rate is just the discount factor wearing a yield costume. A zero-coupon bond's YTM is the spot rate for its maturity — the two are the same number.

When strips are unavailable you extract the curve from coupon bonds by bootstrapping: price the shortest bond to get d(0.5), substitute it into the next bond to get d(1.0), and keep going.

Forward rates

The rate you can lock in today for borrowing or lending over a future period. It is the rate that makes two strategies indifferent: hold an n-period zero to maturity, or hold an (n−1)-period zero and roll the proceeds through period n.

Three theories of the shape

Future short rates are not known today. Given a curve, what is the market's best guess of them?

1

Market segmentation

Each maturity bucket has its own supply and demand — pension funds at the long end, banks at the short end — and clears separately. The shape carries no forecast at all.
2

Expectations

E[r̃n] = fn
The forward rate is the market's expectation. Shape is determined solely by expected future rates: upward slope means the market expects rates to rise.
3

Liquidity preference

fn = E[r̃n] + premium
Holding a bond whose maturity differs from your horizon is risk, and risk is compensated. Lenders are short-term, borrowers long-term; the premium bribes lenders to stretch. Generally positive and fairly stable — which is why the curve usually slopes up even with flat expectations.

TuckmanCh. 2

What an unchanged curve does to a bond as it ages

Value 1.445% fixed legs of different maturities on the table above: 0.5y 100.37 · 1y 100.57 · 1.5y 100.60 · 2y 100.41 · 2.5y 100.00. The value rises, then falls. Why?

Adding one more six-month period to a bond adds value if its coupon beats the forward rate for that period, and subtracts value if it does not. 1.445% beats the 1.046% and 1.384% forwards, so extending from 0.5 to 1.5 years adds value; it falls short of the 1.820% and 2.301% forwards, so extending beyond 1.5 years subtracts it. Turn it around: if nothing moves, a bond's price drifts up over the next six months when its coupon is below the forward rate for its final six-month period, and down when its coupon is above it. FabozziCh. 5

Forward rates are hedgeable rates, not forecasts

With a 5.25% six-month spot and a 5.50% one-year spot, the six-month rate six months forward is 5.75%. An investor with a one-year horizon who expects the six-month rate to rise to 5.60% might roll six-month bills. That is the wrong call: rolling only wins if the future rate beats 5.75%, and 5.60% does not. Buy the one-year.

Studies find forward rates predict future rates poorly. That does not make them useless. The forward tells you the break-even your own view has to beat, and buying the longer security locks it in — which is why some practitioners avoid calling forwards "the market's consensus" and call them hedgeable rates.

FabozziCh. 5

The shape theories, sharpened

  • Pure expectations has three readings: every maturity strategy earns the same over any horizon (too strong — it ignores price risk); the local version, where all bonds earn the same over the next short period (the only one consistent with equilibrium); and return-to-maturity, where rolling short bonds matches a zero to the horizon.
  • Liquidity theory: forwards = expectations + a premium that rises with maturity, so an upward curve is possible even if rates are expected to fall.
  • Preferred habitat: the premium can be positive or negative depending on supply and demand in each maturity range — so any shape is possible.
  • Market segmentation: nobody leaves their sector at any price; each segment clears on its own.

Ilmanen's empirical work finds three influences on the actual Treasury curve: rate expectations, bond risk premia (rising steeply out to about 3 years of duration, slowly after), and convexity bias — long bonds are more convex, investors pay for that, and so long yields sit lower than they otherwise would.

Vocabulary: a positively sloped curve with a 6-month to 30-year spread of 300 bp or less is normal, above 300 bp steep. August 1981 was the textbook inversion: 2-year 16.91%, 30-year 13.95%. A widening spread is a steepening, a narrowing one a flattening — even when both ends move in the same direction.

Prices, quotes, YTM and what you actually earned

The curve prices the bond. The calendar decides what you hand over. YTM compresses it all into one number — usefully, and dangerously.

Pricing off the curve

P = Σt Ct(1 + rt)t   (annual rates, t in years)   =   Σt Ct(1 + rt/2)2t   (semiannual) Each cash flow gets its own rate. A 10% 2-year bond on the standard curve: 5/1.06090.5 + 5/1.0650 + 5/1.06561.5 + 105/1.07002 = 105.7975 per 100 — identical to the semiannual version, because they are the same discount factors written two ways.

Trading between coupon dates

The quoted price assumes the next coupon is exactly one full period away. Trade mid-period and it is not, so two adjustments appear at once: the exponents shrink by the fraction of the period already elapsed, and the seller is owed their share of the coming coupon.

Invoice (dirty) price = quote (clean) price + accrued interest   where   AI = XY × coupon X = days from the last coupon up to but excluding the settlement date. Y = days in the coupon period. Note the count runs to settlement, not to the trade date — buy on Thursday Oct 29 and settlement is Friday Oct 30 (X = 76); buy on Friday Oct 30 and settlement rolls to Monday Nov 2 (X = 79).

A 4% semiannual bond quoted 100:01, last coupon Aug 15, Y = 184, settling Oct 30 with X = 76: 100.03125 + (76/184)(2) = 100.8573. The clean price is what is quoted and what you compare across bonds; the dirty price is what leaves your account.

Half of a bond's return can be interest on interest

TuckmanCh. 3

Yield is not a measure of relative value

Price a zero, a par bond and a 9%-coupon bond of the same maturity off the same discount factors, then compute their yields. On a rising curve, zero yields > par yields > 9%-coupon yields — all three fairly priced. This is the coupon effect: a high-coupon bond's yield is a blend that leans on the lower short-term spot rates.

Two consequences. "The yield curve" is undefined until you say whose cash flows. And a higher yield does not mean a better investment. Yield remains useful as a way to quote price; it is not a way to compare bonds.

Japan quotes government bonds on a simple yield: y = c/p + (1 − p)/(p·T). A 2% 20-year JGB at 101.45 yields 1.90%.

Duration, convexity, and the price of a basis point

Price and yield are related by a curve, not a line. Duration is the tangent; convexity is the correction; the gap between them is why traders care.

Four facts about option-free bonds

  • Price and yield move in opposite directions.
  • The relation is convex — the gain from a rate fall exceeds the loss from an equal rate rise.
  • Longer maturity ⇒ more sensitive, all else equal.
  • Lower coupon ⇒ more sensitive, all else equal. (A zero is the extreme case: all of its weight sits at maturity.)

PVBP / DV01

PVBP = price(y) − price(y + 0.01%)   →   Δprice = − PVBP × (change in bp) Dollar value of one basis point. For $1,000 of the 5-year zero at 5%: 781.20 − 780.82 = $0.38097. Ten bp lower ⇒ +$3.81 (2% off the truth). A hundred bp lower ⇒ +$38.10 (2.7% off). It degrades quickly because it is a straight line.

Simple, and directly usable for hedging — but it scales with position size rather than describing the bond, it is symmetric so it ignores convexity entirely, and like everything here it assumes a flat curve shifting in parallel.

Duration

Dmac = 1P Σ t · Ct(1+y)t     Dmod = Dmac1 + y     dPP = − Dmod · dy Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time to the cash flows — measured in periods, so divide by 2 for years. Modified duration is what you actually use: the percentage price change per unit change in yield. A zero's Macaulay duration is exactly its maturity.

The 5-year zero at 5%: Dmac = 10 half-years = 5 years, Dmod = 5/1.025 = 4.878. A 100bp fall predicts +4.878%, or $38.10 on a price of 781.20 — against a true $39.15. Duration always understates the gain and overstates the loss, because the true curve bends away from the tangent on both sides.

Portfolio duration is the value-weighted sum of the components' durations — which is the property that makes it the working risk number on a desk. Same for convexity.

Convexity

C = 1P Σ t(t+1) Ct(1+y)t+2   (÷ m² for years)     dPP = − Dmod dy + ½ C (dy)² The second-order term of the Taylor expansion. Positive for every option-free bond, and larger the lower the coupon. Add it back to the 5-year zero: −4.878(−0.01) + ½(26.175)(0.0001) = 5.009% → $39.13 against a true $39.15. Two terms is enough.

Hedging with DV01 — and why the hedge is only local

FB = FA × DV01ADV01B A market maker short $100M of a futures option (DV01 0.03505 per 100) buys futures with DV01 0.07442: 100M × 0.03505/0.07442 = $47.098M. The higher-DV01 instrument is traded in the smaller size.

After a 5 bp move the book barely changes. After 25 bp it is off by about $150,000 — whichever way rates went — against roughly $15,625 earned selling the option half a tick rich. The position is short convexity: the option's DV01 changes faster than the futures', so the hedge must be rebalanced (sell futures as rates rise, buy as they fall). Long convexity is long volatility. It wins when rates move a lot, and pays for that through the premium when they sit still.

Scale also matters: the futures has duration 6.2, the option 201.6. For an investor putting the same dollars into each, the option is thirty times as rate-sensitive.

DV01 and duration move differently across bonds

DV01 = P × D10,000 2⅛s of May 2015 at 2.092%: price 100.1559, DV01 0.04728, duration 4.72, convexity 25.29. The final payment alone contributes 95.3% of the risk. A five-year zero at the same yield has convexity 26.93 — a coupon bond is less convex than a zero of the same maturity.
  • Raising the coupon lowers duration (more value arrives early) but raises DV01 (the bond is worth more).
  • A zero's duration rises in a straight line with maturity, but its DV01 eventually falls toward zero as its price collapses.
  • Long bonds converge on a perpetuity, whose duration is 1/y: 28.6 at 3.5%, whatever the coupon.

Coupon 07 · Interest rate futures

Locking in rates you don't have yet

A futures contract fixes today the price of something delivered later. On interest rates that means an index that moves opposite to rates, a Treasury contract on a bond that doesn't exist, and a short who holds every delivery choice.

The contract, the exchange, the margin

A futures contract obliges one party to buy and the other to sell at the futures price on a set settlement date. It trades on an exchange, and the clearinghouse becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, so you never carry your counterparty's credit risk and can close a position by taking the offsetting one.

You post initial margin as good-faith money, positions are marked to market daily, and if equity falls below the maintenance margin you must top it back up with cash (variation margin). Margin is a fraction of the contract's value, so futures are heavily leveraged. Fewer than 2% of contracts end in delivery — most are closed out first.

Cheapest to deliver, and the short's options

The short picks the issue that costs least to deliver. Receiving futures price × CF for a bond that costs its quoted price, the cost of delivery is price − futures × CF, and the lowest wins. Futures versus forwards

A forward is negotiated over the counter, usually held to delivery, and not necessarily marked to market, so each side carries the other's credit risk. Futures are standardised, cleared and settled daily. For interest rate contracts that daily settlement matters, because the margin cash flows are correlated with the rate the contract is on: a short futures position receives cash when rates rise (and reinvests it at higher rates) and pays when rates fall (and finances it cheaply). The adjustment the deck gives:

forward rate = futures rate − ½ σ² t1 t2 t1 = time to the futures' maturity, t2 = maturity of the underlying rate (90 days later), σ = standard deviation of short-rate changes per year (about 0.012). With t1 = 8 and t2 = 8.25: ½(0.012²)(8)(8.25) = 0.475%, so an 8-year futures rate of 6.00% implies a forward rate near 5.52%.

Pricing: cash and carry

A 20-year 12% bond sells at 100 and is deliverable in 3 months; the 3-month financing rate is 8%. If the futures trade at 107: sell them, borrow 100 and buy the bond. At delivery you receive 107 + 3 of accrued coupon and repay 102 — +8 with no money down. At 92, run it in reverse (buy futures, short the bond, lend 100): +7. Only 99 leaves nothing on the table.

F = P [1 + t (r − c)]    100[1 + 0.25(0.08 − 0.12)] = 99r = financing rate, c = current yield (coupon ÷ price). r − c is the cost of carry. Positive carry (c > r, typical of an upward-sloping curve) puts futures below the cash price; an inverted curve puts them above. If you borrow at 8% but lend at only 6%, arbitrage pins F between 98.50 and 99.

Delivery options belong to the short, so the long pays less: F = P[1 + t(r − c)] − value of the delivery options. The model's other simplifications — no margin flows, one known deliverable and date — explain why futures and forward prices differ.

Adjusting duration with futures

Buying futures adds dollar duration; selling removes it. A $48.11M portfolio with duration 2.97 against a benchmark at 3.68 needs 3.68% − 2.97% of its value, about $341,516 of extra dollar duration per 100 bp. A 5-year note futures contract carries about $5,022, so the manager buys 68 ($6.8M notional). Targeting 90% of the benchmark (3.31) needs 33. A full hedge has target duration zero: short about 284.

Hedging one bond: PVBP, conversion factor, yield beta

To hedge a bond you don't deliver (a cross hedge), match dollar volatilities at the date the hedge is lifted. The futures move like the CTD divided by its conversion factor, so:

hedge ratio = PVBP of bond hedgedPVBP of CTD × CF of CTD × yield beta    contracts = ratio × par hedged / contract parP&G 5.55% of 2037, $10M: 0.1363/0.1207 × 1.0246 = 1.157 → 116 contracts. A regression yield beta of about 0.90 cuts that to about 104 (the book prints 105). The pure-volatility alternative, the ratio of yield-change standard deviations, gave 1.01–1.08 depending on the window.

A hedge swaps price risk for basis risk: the spread to the CTD can change, and so can the CTD itself. Fabozzi's P&G hedge still lands within about 1% of its $9.678M target across a wide range of rates. A short hedge (sell futures) protects a future sale; a long hedge (buy futures) locks in a purchase price for cash arriving later.

FabozziCh. 29

Synthetic securities

Own a 20-year Treasury and sell futures for delivery in three months, and you've locked the sale price: together that's a synthetic 3-month T-bill. Rearranged, a cash bond equals a T-bill plus long futures, and long futures equal a cash bond bought with borrowed money — the cash-and-carry trade again. If the synthetic bill yields more than the real one, the difference is yield enhancement.

riskless short-term position = cash bond position − futures bond position
